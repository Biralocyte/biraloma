### Management decisions
- "Unstable but reducible OO requires an occipitocervical or atlantoaxial fusion, depending on the levels of instability. Irreducible OO is treated with intraoperative O-arm reduction. [dlouhy p.2]
- "If OO is irreducible with flexion or extension, skeletal traction, intraoperative O-arm reduction, or posterior intraoperative distraction and reduction, a ventral decompression is required prior to posterior fusion." [dlouhy p.2]

## Anatomy & ligaments
- "The transverse ligament is a tight band positioned to trap the OP against the anterior arch of C-1, thereby allowing physiological rotation of C-1 on C-2 and, at the same time, providing stability by preventing translational motion of C-1 on C-2 that could cause spinal cord injury." [dlouhy p.5]
- "The transverse ligament represents the primary restraint to translational motion of C-1 with respect to C-2 because the atlantoaxial joint has flat lateral mass articulations, no intervertebral disc to limit motion, loose capsular ligaments, weak posterior ligaments, and lack of the ligamentum flavum." [dlouhy p.5]
- "With OO, the lack of continuity of the ossicle with the body of C-2 hinders the ability of the transverse ligament to limit translational movement of C-1 on the body of C-2." [dlouhy p.5-6]
- "Because the gap between the ossicle and the body of C-2 usually extends above the level of the superior facets of C-2, over time this leads to incompetence of the transverse cruciate ligament and subsequently, C1–2 (atlantoaxial) instability and dislocation." [dlouhy p.2]
- "Continued stress on the C1–2 joint, either with normal physiological movements of the neck, abrupt trauma, physical stress, or a predisposition to ligamentous instability can lead to incompetence of the transverse ligament and gross radiographic instability." [dlouhy p.6]
- Tectorial membrane: "lies in its normal position" (Figs 1, 3) [dlouhy p.4, 6]

## Imaging technique
- Pre-1984: "all patients underwent plain cervical radiography and pluridirectional tomography in the flexed, extended, and lateral bending positions. This was supplemented with CT imaging, CT myelography, vertebral angiography when necessary, and documentation of the effects of cervical traction. After 1984, CT myelography was replaced with MRI, and vertebral angiography was replaced with CT angiography." [dlouhy p.2]
- Today: CVJ CT and flexion-extension MRI in all patients; 3D CT since 1990 [dlouhy p.2]. Figs show T2-weighted sagittal flexion/extension/neutral MRI, T1 MRI (Fig 4), sagittal/coronal CT, halo traction radiograph [dlouhy p.4-7].
- "Reducibility pertains to the reestablishment of anatomical alignment to relieve compression of neural structures." [dlouhy p.2]

## Congenital/developmental
- "Os odontoideum (OO) is a rare craniovertebral junction (CVJ) abnormality in which an ossicle (small bone) of varying size and shape is separated from a hypoplastic odontoid process (OP) by a variable gap. The ossicle is cranial to the hypoplastic dens, has rounded and smooth cortical borders, and has no osseous continuity with the hypoplastic dens or the body of C-2." [dlouhy p.2]
- "Orthotopic OO is defined as an ossicle lying in the normal position of the typical OP and that moves with C-1 or in conjunction with the body of C-2. Dystopic OO is defined as an ossicle that is located near the occipital bone and that may be physically or functionally fused with the basion of the clivus, and that therefore moves separately from the body of C-2." [dlouhy p.8]
- Origin: "Some have suggested OO is a congenital and/or developmental abnormality, which occurs after failure of the center of ossification of the dens to fuse with the body of C-2. This is supported by familial and identical twin cases of OO" [dlouhy p.5]; "Others have proposed that OO may represent an unremembered and/or unrecognized fracture through the C-2/dens growth plate in childhood (nonhealed odontoid synchondrosis fracture) leading to a chronic nonunited fracture of the OP." [dlouhy p.5]; "It is likely that OO occurs more commonly from posttraumatic origins, with some cases being congenital. Additionally, there may also be cases that occur via both mechanisms." [dlouhy p.5]
- Associated (Table 1, n=41): Chiari I 3 (7%); basilar invagination 6 (15%); atlas assimilation 6 (15%). Subaxial segmentation failure: 5 of 16 pediatric (31%), 0 of 25 adults. [dlouhy p.2-3]
- Case 3: "dystopic OO, which was attached to the lower clivus"; ventral mid-medulla compression; "cerebellar tonsillar ectopia, with the tonsils extending below the posterior arch of C-1 and the cervicomedullary buckle identified between the C-1 and C-2 vertebral body level." Postoperative: "ventral medulla was decompressed and the cerebellar tonsils had ascended." [dlouhy p.5]

## Trauma & classifications
- Anatomical classification: orthotopic vs dystopic (above) [dlouhy p.8]; "As with all CVJ abnormalities, OO has been categorized based on its instability and reducibility." [dlouhy p.2]
- Posttraumatic hypothesis "supported by case reports of patients with a previously documented intact C-2 who later were found to have OO after remote trauma." [dlouhy p.5]
- Case 2 history of trauma at age 3 [dlouhy p.3-4].

## Syndromic
- "Down syndrome results in ligamentous laxity and predisposes to Oc–C1 and C1–2 instability. Therefore, it's likely that the already lax transverse ligament in cases of Down syndrome and OO is more susceptible to ultimately being fixed anterior to the ossicle in OO." [dlouhy p.7-8]
- "Of the 16 pediatric patients, 6 had Down syndrome (38%), whereas of the 25 adult patients, none did." [dlouhy p.2]
- "Similar to Down syndrome, segmentation failure of the subaxial spine is also associated with instability at C1–2 because it places more stress on the C1–2 joint for normal physiological movements, predisposing to C1–2 instability and then irreducibility." [dlouhy p.8]
- Opsismodysplasia siblings cited in refs (ref 1). [dlouhy p.8]
