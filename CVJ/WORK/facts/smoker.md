# Facts: smoker (Smoker WRK, RadioGraphics 1994;14:255-277). Quotes verbatim (OCR hyphenation/spacing fixed). Note source spells "Welcher basal angle".

## Anatomy & ligaments
- "The craniovertebral (or craniocervical) junction (CVJ) is a collective term that refers to the occiput (posterior skull base), atlas, axis, and supporting ligaments. It encloses the soft-tissue structures of the cervicomedullary junction (medulla, spinal cord, and lower cranial nerves)." [smoker p.2]
- "The occipital bone is composed of basioccipital, exoccipital, and supraoccipital portions enclosing the foramen magnum (Fig 9a)." [smoker p.8]
- "The basiocciput, embryologically derived from fusion of four occipital sclerotomes (also referred to as primary cranial vertebrae), forms the lower portion of the clivus (Fig 9b)." [smoker p.8]
- "The upper portion of the clivus is formed by the basisphenoid, separated from the basiocciput by the sphenooccipital synchondrosis." [smoker p.8]
- Synchondrosis fusion age: "The age at which this synchondrosis fuses, as reported in the literature, ranges from 'after the twelfth year' (1) to 14-16 years for girls and 16-18.5 years for boys (2,9)." [smoker p.8-9]
- Landmarks (lateral, Fig 2): "nasion (1), tuberculum sella (2), basion (anterior margin of the foramen magnum) (3), opisthion (posterior margin of the foramen magnum) (4), posterior pole of the hard palate (5), anterior arch of the atlas (6), posterior arch of the atlas (7), and odontoid process (8)." [smoker p.4]
- Landmarks (coronal, Fig 3): "occipital condyles (1), lateral masses of the atlas (2), odontoid process (3), axis body (4), and tips of the mastoid processes (5)". [smoker p.4]
- "Some of these anatomic landmarks (eg, nasion, tuberculum, hard palate) require views of the skull and not views of the cervical spine." [smoker p.4]
- Normal anterior atlas arch on lateral radiograph: "the anterior arch of the atlas appears crescentic or half-moon-shaped, with dense cortical bone surrounding the medullary cavity and a well-defined predental space (anterior atlantoaxial interval)" (Fig 23a: "1-2-mm predental space"). [smoker p.16-17]
- Atlas and axis ossification centers shown in Figs 20 and 26 (diagrams only). [smoker p.14, 18]
- "Because the posterior arch of the atlas lacks a true spinous process, the often-used term 'spina bifida of the atlas' is incorrect, and use of the term 'posterior atlas arch rachischisis' is encouraged." [smoker p.15]
- Arch-canal line: "Absence of the arch-canal line, which typically falls on the spinolaminar line, is usually easily identified on lateral plain radiographs in which the two complete posterior arches may be seen in their entirety." [smoker p.15]

## Imaging technique
- "With the virtual extinction of polytomography in many institutions, detailed evaluation of this region fell to the realm of computed tomography (CT) or CT myelography. Unfortunately, adequate evaluation of the CVJ necessitates coronal and sagittal images, which, with CT, are available only as reconstructed images. This latter technique requires a large number of overlapping, thin-section axial images and an extremely cooperative patient." [smoker p.2]
- "Now, with the widespread availability of magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, the CVJ is seen in virtually every sagittal MR imaging study of the brain." [smoker p.2]
- "Over the years, multiple lines, planes, and angles have been described for assessment of CVJ relationships, initially with radiography and later with polytomography." [smoker p.4]
- "There is a certain disadvantage in all of these measurements because the anatomic structures and planes vary within a normal range (1). In all cases, more than one measurement should be assessed." [smoker p.4]
- "Two lines have remained particularly useful for evaluation of CVJ relationships with virtually any imaging modality: the Chamberlain line and the Wackenheim clivus baseline. Although used less frequently, two angles also continue to be useful: the Welcher basal angle and the atlantooccipital joint axis angle." [smoker p.4]
- Platybasia / clivus-canal angle: "It is best assessed on midsagittal MR images or lateral views of the skull." [smoker p.8]
- Split atlas: "Although the anterior rachischisis may occasionally be identified on a submentovertex view of the skull, it is easily appreciated on axial CT scans (Figs 24, 25)." [smoker p.17]
- Posterior arch rachischisis on open-mouth view: "may be superimposed on the odontoid process or the axis body on the open-mouth odontoid view, simulating a fracture (Fig 22). Obtaining a few axial CT scans can easily solve this dilemma" [smoker p.15]
- Basiocciput hypoplasia: "It is frequently impossible to identify the tip of the elevated odontoid process on lateral radiographs, although the presence of basilar invagination is usually obvious. These abnormal relationships, as well as the causative basiocciput hypoplasia, are all easily appreciated on midsagittal MR images (Figs 14, 15)." [smoker p.11]
- Os odontoideum instability: "The degree of instability must be assessed by comparison of flexion and extension studies with plain radiography, polytomography, or MR imaging (Fig 30)" [smoker p.20]
- Summary: "accurate assessment of the CVJ is possible, with the liberal use of multiple imaging modalities and orthogonal planes." [smoker p.22]
- MR sequences in figures: T1-weighted (600/20, 700/20), proton density (3,000/45) (Figs 2-8, 31). [smoker p.4-20]

## Craniometry
- Chamberlain line. Definition: "The Chamberlain line extends between the posterior pole of the hard palate and the opisthion (posterior margin of the foramen magnum) (Fig 4) (3)." Shown on radiograph and midsagittal T1 MR. Normal: "The tip of the odontoid process commonly lies below or just tangent to the Chamberlain line, and it may normally project above this line for a distance of several millimeters. The maximum distance that the odontoid process may be seen above this line is variably reported in the literature, ranging from 1 mm ± 3.6 to 6.6 mm (1-5)." "The anterior arch of the atlas typically lies below these lines." Abnormal: violation = basilar invagination. [smoker p.5]
- McGregor line: "McGregor (4) developed a modification of the Chamberlain line when the opisthion could not be identified on plain radiographs. This McGregor line extends between the posterior pole of the hard palate and the lowest point of the occipital squamosal surface. ... The odontoid tip may be slightly higher if the McGregor line is used." [smoker p.5]
- Wackenheim clivus baseline (basilar line): "constructed by drawing a line along the clivus and extrapolating it inferiorly into the upper cervical spinal canal (Fig 5). This line should fall tangent to the posterior aspect of the tip of the odontoid process (6)." (Figure 30 legend: line "falls tangent to the anterior aspect of the os" in os odontoideum.) Defined on radiograph and MR (Fig 5). [smoker p.7, 21]
- Clivus-canal (craniovertebral) angle: "The angle formed at the intersection of the Wackenheim clivus baseline with a line constructed along the posterior surface of the axis body and odontoid process (craniovertebral or clivus-canal angle) normally ranges from 150 degrees in flexion to 180 degrees in extension (Fig 5) (2). Ventral spinal cord compression may occur when the angle is less than 150 degrees (2)." Fig 5 legend: "should range between 150 and 180." [smoker p.7, 6]
- Clivus-canal angle in platybasia/invagination: "Normally 150 degrees or greater, this angle may approach 90 degrees in some patients, producing bow-string deformity and marked compression of the cervicomedullary junction." Fig 8: "the clivus-canal angle measures 110 degrees". [smoker p.8]
- Welcher basal angle: "formed at the intersection of the nasion-tuberculum line and the tuberculum-basion line (Fig 6). It averages 132 degrees and should always be less than 140 degrees (1,7). This angle is increased when the skull base is abnormally flattened." Fig 6 legend: "should be 140 degrees or less." Defined on lateral radiograph and midsagittal MR. Examples: 155 degrees (Fig 8), 150 degrees (Fig 10). [smoker p.7, 6, 8, 9]
- Atlantooccipital joint axis angle: "formed by lines drawn parallel to the atlantooccipital joints, which typically intersect at the center of the odontoid process when the condyles are symmetric (Fig 7) (2). The average angle is 125 degrees, with a range between 124 degrees and 127 degrees (1). This angle becomes more obtuse in the presence of occipital condyle hypoplasia." Shown on coronal CT and coronal MR. [smoker p.7]
- Bimastoid line: "If the mastoid tips were connected (bimastoid line), the entirety of the odontoid process would lie below this line. The skull base should descend as it approaches midline (arrows)." "Whereas the tip of the odontoid process and the lateral masses of the atlas typically lie below a line connecting the mastoid tips (bimastoid line), this relationship is violated in condylar hypoplasia." [smoker p.7, 10]
- Anterior atlantodental interval: only qualitative: "There is a marked increase in the anterior atlantodental interval (dotted line)" (Fig 19b); no normal value given. [smoker p.14]
- Predental (anterior atlantoaxial) space: "1-2-mm predental space" normal (Fig 23a). [smoker p.16]
- No Powers ratio, ADI cut-off, McRae line, Redlund-Johnell, Ranawat, Clark, Fischgold etc. in this source.

## Terminology (definitions)
- "The literature describing the CVJ continues to fuel the confusion related to this region, with the frequent, erroneous, interchangeable use of the terms 'basilar invagination,' 'basilar impression,' and 'platybasia.' These terms are not synonymous." [smoker p.8]
- Basilar invagination: "refers to a primary developmental anomaly in which the vertebral column is abnormally high and prolapsed into the skull base (2). Because the anomaly may be due to a number of causes (basiocciput hypoplasia, occipital condyle hypoplasia, various atlantooccipital assimilations), it might be best to think of basilar invagination as a radiographic finding and not a diagnosis in and of itself. If at all possible, one should attempt to define the underlying abnormality responsible for the basilar invagination." [smoker p.8]
- "There is an increased prevalence of neural dysgenesis associated with basilar invagination, such as the Chiari malformation or syringohydromyelia, reported to occur in 25%-35% of these patients (2)." [smoker p.8]
- Basilar impression: "should be reserved for the secondary or acquired form of basilar invagination. It results from softening of the skull base and is uncommon, mainly seen in association with Paget disease and osteomalacia (1). Other conditions that have been described in association with basilar impression include hyperparathyroidism, osteogenesis imperfecta, Hurler syndrome, rickets, and skull base infection (2)." [smoker p.8]
- "The term 'cranial settling' is typically applied to the CVJ changes associated with rheumatoid arthritis (8)." [smoker p.8]
- Platybasia: "an anthropometric term that refers to flattening of the skull base, manifested by an increase in the Welcher basal angle. Platybasia may occasionally be an isolated finding without associated basilar invagination (2). More often, however, basilar invagination is present." "Although the Chamberlain line and Wackenheim clivus baseline may not be violated, the craniovertebral or clivus-canal angle becomes abnormally acute (Fig 8)." [smoker p.8]

## Congenital/developmental
- Summary: "Most anomalies of the occiput are associated with decreased skull base height and basilar invagination" [smoker p.1, 9]; "Most atlas anomalies produce no abnormal CVJ relationships and are not associated with basilar invagination"; "Except for fusion anomalies, abnormalities of the axis are primarily confined to the odontoid process and are not associated with basilar invagination." [smoker p.1]
- Condylus tertius: "When the hypochordal bow of the fourth occipital sclerotome (proatlas) persists or when the proatlas fails to integrate, an ossified remnant may be present at the distal end of the clivus, called the condylus tertius or third occipital condyle (1,2,5). Although typically single, multiple supernumerary ossicles may be present (Fig 10) (9). This third condyle may form a joint or pseudojoint with the odontoid process or with the anterior arch of the atlas and may lead to limitation in the range of motion of the CVJ (1). There is an increased prevalence of os odontoideum associated with this abnormality (2)." [smoker p.9]
- Condylar hypoplasia: "the occipital condyles are underdeveloped and have a flattened appearance, leading to basilar invagination (violation of the Chamberlain line) and widening of the atlantooccipital joint axis angle (Fig 11a)." "the skull base is flattened or even ascends medially (Figs 11, 12)." "The lateral masses of the atlas may be fused to the hypoplastic condyles, further accentuating the basilar invagination. Clinically, condylar hypoplasia limits, or may even abolish, movements at the atlantooccipital joint and may occasionally lead to compression of the vertebral artery secondary to excessive posterior gliding of the occiput in relation to the atlas (10)." Fig 12b: sphenoid sinus, clivus, anterior atlas arch and odontoid all on the same axial section: "markedly abnormal". [smoker p.10]
- Basiocciput hypoplasia: "may be mild or severe, depending on the number of occipital vertebrae affected. It results in shortening of the clivus and violation of the Chamberlain line and is virtually always associated with basilar invagination (Fig 13) (1,9). The Wackenheim clivus baseline is usually normal, although the clivus-canal angle is typically decreased and there may be bow-string deformity of the cervicomedullary junction." Fig 14/15: basion at the middle of the pons. [smoker p.10-11]
- Atlantooccipital assimilation: "The failure of segmentation between the skull and first cervical vertebra results in assimilation of the atlas. The assimilation may be complete (Figs 16, 17) or partial. It invariably results in basilar invagination. Although the Wackenheim clivus baseline may be normal, the clivus-canal angle may be decreased. When incompletely assimilated, the atlas arches appear too high on the lateral plain radiograph or, when completely assimilated, are not visible at all (Fig 18)." [smoker p.12]
- "There is an increased prevalence of associated fusion of the axis and third cervical vertebra in association with atlantooccipital assimilation (11). When this is present, gradual loosening of the atlantodental joint with progressive atlantoaxial subluxation may occur, reported in approximately 50% of cases (Fig 19) (1,2). In some instances, atlantooccipital assimilation may be associated with sudden death (12)." [smoker p.12]
- Clinical picture of AO assimilation: "short broad neck, elevation of the scapula, low hairline, and limitation of neck movement (2). The anomaly may also be associated with abnormalities of the jaw, incomplete clefting of nasal cartilages, cleft palate, ear deformities, cervical ribs, hypospadias, and urinary tract anomalies (2)." [smoker p.2]
- Atlas posterior arch aplasia: "Total or partial aplasia of the posterior atlas arch is rare (13,14). ... including total aplasia, Keller-type aplasia with persistence of the posterior tubercle, aplasia with a unilateral or bilateral remnant and midline rachischisis, and hemiaplasia or partial hemiaplasia of the posterior arch (Fig 21) (1,13). Although absence of the posterior arch, when isolated, has been stated to be asymptomatic (15), reports of associated anterior atlantoaxial subluxation have been made (14), and bilateral atlantoaxial offset has been reported in association with both total and partial aplasias, simulating the Jefferson fracture (13)." [smoker p.14]
- Arch anomalies vs fracture: "arch anomalies are, not infrequently, mistaken for fractures in the evaluation of plain radiographs of patients with a history of cervical spine trauma." [smoker p.14] Fig 21: "initially mistaken for a C-1 arch fracture ... both 'arches' are intact and completely corticated." [smoker p.15]
- Atlas clefts: "Posterior rachischisis, the most common, is observed in 4% of adult autopsy specimens (13). The vast majority of posterior atlas clefts (97%) are midline, whereas lateral clefts, through the sulcus of the vertebral artery, account for the remaining 3%." [smoker p.14-15]
- Split atlas: "anterior arch rachischisis is quite rare, occurring in 0.1% of autopsy specimens (1). It is typically encountered in association with posterior rachischisis, in which case the term 'split atlas' should be applied (6)." Radiograph: anterior arch "appears fat or plump and rounded in configuration, appearing to 'overlap' the odontoid process (making identification of the predental space impossible); the arch may have unsharp, duplicated anterior margins (Fig 23b)". MR: "the midsagittal T1-weighted image reveals an absence of the normal marrow signal intensity at the anterior arch level and a large, rounded, cortical signal void where the anterior arch should be (anterior arch pseudotumor) (Fig 25d)." "To this author's knowledge, the appearance of a split atlas on MR images has not been described." [smoker p.16-18]
- "Although clefts in the arch of the axis [sic: atlas context] are frequently incidental findings, atlas clefts may indicate a dysraphic anomaly of the spinal cord or meninges, and wide clefts with only a fibrous covering may lead to atlas instability (1)." [smoker p.18]
- Persistent ossiculum terminale (Bergman ossicle): "results from failure of fusion of the terminal ossicle to the remainder of the odontoid process (Fig 27); the fusion typically is accomplished by 12 years of age (2). On occasion, Bergman ossicle may be confused with a type 1 odontoid fracture (avulsion of the terminal ossicle), and absolute differentiation between the two diagnoses may be difficult (21). Whether traumatic or congenital in origin, this anomaly is stable when isolated and of relatively little clinical significance. The odontoid process is usually normal in height." [smoker p.18-19]
- Odontoid aplasia: "Total aplasia of the odontoid process is extremely rare (Fig 28) (22). Not infrequently, an os odontoideum may simulate odontoid aplasia, as the os fragment may be perfectly projected over the atlas arch on the open-mouth odontoid view of the upper cervical spine (Fig 29)." [smoker p.19]
- Os odontoideum: "refers to an independent osseous structure lying cephalad to the axis body in the location of the odontoid process (Fig 30) (23)" (term "first introduced by Giacomini in 1886"). "The anterior arch of the atlas may appear rounded and hypertrophic, situated too far posterior in relation to the body of the axis (Fig 31) (24)." "Because the gap between the os odontoideum and the axis body usually extends above the level of the superior articular facet of the axis, cruciate ligament incompetence and atlantoaxial instability are common (25)." "When present, instability may lead to substantial narrowing of the spinal canal and cord compression at the level of C-1." [smoker p.20]

## Trauma & classifications
- Os odontoideum vs type 2 odontoid fracture: "In the presence of an os odontoideum, the axis body has a well-corticated convex upper margin, and the anterior atlas arch appears hypertrophic and rounded, rather than half-moon-shaped (Fig 32). In contrast, the type 2 odontoid fracture is typically associated with a flattened, sharp, uncorticated margin to the upper axis body and a normal, half-moon-shaped appearance to the anterior atlas arch (Fig 32)." Fig 32b: "The odontoid and atlas are posteriorly displaced." [smoker p.20-22]
- Odontoid fracture types: only "type 1" (avulsion of the terminal ossicle) and "type 2" are mentioned, citing Anderson and D'Alonzo (ref 21: "Anderson LD, D'Alonzo RT. Fractures of the odontoid process of the axis. J Bone Joint Surg [Am] 1974; 56:1663-1674"); the classification itself is not described. [smoker p.18, 20, 23]
- Posterior arch aplasia can simulate Jefferson fracture (see above). No Traynelis, Levine-Edwards, Landells, Tuli/Fielding, Goel in this source.

## Rheumatoid/inflammatory
- "The term 'cranial settling' is typically applied to the CVJ changes associated with rheumatoid arthritis (8)." Reference: El Khoury et al, Cranial settling in rheumatoid arthritis, Radiology 1980. No other rheumatoid content. [smoker p.8, 23]

## Syndromic
- Hurler syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta, rickets, hyperparathyroidism listed with basilar impression (above). [smoker p.8] No Down syndrome, Morquio, or achondroplasia.

## Degenerative/other
- Paget disease and osteomalacia: basilar impression "mainly seen in association with Paget disease and osteomalacia". [smoker p.8]
- Signs and symptoms table (p2): Motor myelopathy ("May be quite subtle and nonspecific"; "May be manifest only by lack of endurance"; "May be quadriparesis, triparesis, paraparesis, hemiparesis, or monoparesis"); Sensory (posterior column dysfunction; hypalgesia (spinothalamic tract dysfunction); bladder dysfunction (urgency or hesitation)); Brain stem dysfunction (nystagmus horizontal or downbeat; apnea; ataxia; dysmetria; internuclear ophthalmoplegia; facial diplegia); Lower cranial nerve dysfunction (decreased hearing; dysphagia; soft palate paralysis; trapezius muscle weakness; tongue atrophy); Vascular compromise (syncope; vertigo; intermittent paresis). [smoker p.2]
- "The signs and symptoms of CVJ abnormalities are varied, typically begin insidiously and arise fairly late, progress slowly, remain stationary, and rarely relapse (1)." "...cranial nerve deficits (eg, vertigo, dysphagia, facial paralysis, decreased hearing, tongue atrophy) may prompt referral to an otolaryngologist." [smoker p.2]

## Instability & management/surgical decision points
- Os odontoideum: instability assessed by flexion/extension (see above); "cruciate ligament incompetence and atlantoaxial instability are common". [smoker p.20]
- AO assimilation with C2-3 fusion: atlantoaxial subluxation "reported in approximately 50% of cases". [smoker p.12]
- Persistent ossiculum terminale: "stable when isolated". [smoker p.19]
- Wide atlas clefts "may lead to atlas instability". [smoker p.18]
- No surgical management discussion (no decompression/fusion/transoral content).
