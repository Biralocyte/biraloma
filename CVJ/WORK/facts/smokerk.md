## Anatomy & ligaments
- "The craniovertebral junction (CVJ) comprises the occiput, atlas, and axis, their articulations and various ligaments. Together, these structures enclose the cervicomedullary junction (CMJ) and the lower four cranial nerves." [p.1; p.1123]
- "The basiocciput, formed by the first two occipital sclerotomes, comprises the inferior half of the clivus, separated from the basisphenoid by the spheno-occipital synchondrosis. The exoccipital bone, which forms the jugular tubercles, arises from the third occipital sclerotome. The anterior tubercle of the clivus is formed by the hypocentrum of the fourth occipital sclerotome (proatlas), while the anterior margin of the foramen magnum, occipital condyles, and the third condyle are formed by the ventral segment of the proatlas neural arch [56]." [p.1-2]
- "The centrum of the first spinal sclerotome forms the odontoid process, whereas the neural arch of this sclerotome forms portions of the C1 arch. The axis body develops from the centrum of the second spinal sclerotome, while the facets and posterior axis arch form from the neural arch of this sclerotome. The terminal portion of the dens forms from the proatlas [56]. This terminal ossicle is not ossified at birth but is usually identified at approximately 3 years of age and fuses with the rest of the dens by 12 years of age. At birth, the odontoid is separated from the axis body by the neural central synchondrosis, present in most children younger than 3-4 years of age, typically disappearing by 8 years of age. This synchondrosis lies below the level of the superior articular facets and does not represent the 'base' of the dens." [p.11-12; p.1133-1134]
- Anterior arch: "The typical anterior C1 arch forms from a single ossification center forming the anterior tubercle, with paramedian synchondroses between the two neural arches (Fig. 13a)." [p.10]. "On a plain lateral radiograph, the normal anterior C1 arch has a crescentic or half-moon configuration, with dense cortical bone surrounding a medullary cavity, and a well-defined anterior atlantodental interval (AADI) [84] (Fig. 14a)." [p.10]
- "The lateral atlantal masses and superior portion of the posterior atlas arch are formed by the caudal division of the neural arch of the proatlas, while the first spinal sclerotome forms the atlas vertebra [56]." [p.8]
- Landmarks (Fig 1): basion/nasion note: see figure catalog (caption prints "A Basion"). Needed view: "Note that a view of the skull/brain is required to include the basion, hard palate, and tuberculum sella, structures often not included on cervical spine examinations." [p.2]
- Ligaments: "Magnetic resonance (MR) imaging is ideal for evaluation of soft tissues, neural structures, and ligaments." [p.1]. Absent/insufficient transverse ligament in block atlas: "Absence or insufficiency of the transverse ligament is typically associated [98]." [p.8]. Hypoplastic/aplastic dens: "the attachments for the apical and alar ligaments are absent, predisposing to atlanto-axial instability and resultant cord compression at the level of the C1 arch [52]" [p.13-14]. Os: "incompetence of the cruciate ligament and atlanto-axial instability are common" [p.12].

## Imaging technique
- "With the widespread use of magnetic resonance imaging, a midsagittal image is available on all brain examinations providing a 'free look' at the CVJ in virtually every patient." [p.1]
- "Multidetector computed tomography (CT) with orthogonal reconstructions optimally defines the osseous anatomy and pathology of the CVJ." [p.1]
- "While some of these measurements may be possible on plain radiographs, superimposition of osseous structures is often problematic for accurate measurements, and CT and MR are optimal." [p.2]
- "All but one of the lines and angles commonly utilized for this purpose are derived from the sagittal view of the skull: Chamberlain's line, Wackenheim's clivus baseline, the clivus-canal angle, and the basal angle (as well as the anterior atlanto-dental interval used in assessment of atlanto-axial instability but not in assessment of basilar invagination or basilar impression)." [p.2]
- "While CT provides optimal visualization of the osseous structures, MR cannot only provide this same information, but also permits assessment of the surrounding soft-tissue structures and CMJ." [p.2]
- Flexion/extension: "The osseous relationships are best demonstrated using flexion and extension plain radiographs or CT (Fig. 15a and b). Flexion and extension MR imaging is also very useful for demonstrating associated cord compression, although obtaining flexion and extension positioning may be difficult in larger patients due to the size of the magnet bore [29]." [p.12]
- Parasagittal CT: "the parasagittal images are ideal for this assessment (Fig. 4)" (occipital condyle height). [p.6]. 3D: Fig 7 caption: "3D Vitrea reconstructed images optimally reveal the non-segmentation and the atlanto-axial subluxation with severe compromise of the osseous foramen magnum." [p.9]

- Terminology: "Platybasia is the term used in reference to flattening of the skull base. It can be considered an index of the position of one portion of the skull base relative to another [51]. Although platybasia may be present in patients with basilar invagination and basilar impression, these conditions may exist in the absence of platybasia." [p.2]
- Definitions: "basilar invagination, a developmental abnormality in which the vertebral column is situated in an abnormally high position due to decreased height of the skull base [28]." Terminology varies: "some authors refer to the deformity as basilar impression when caused by a developmental osseous abnormality and basilar invagination to refer to the deformity resulting from acquired, skull base softening. Some authors use the terms 'primary' and 'secondary/acquired' basilar impression..." [p.2]

## Congenital/developmental
- Atlanto-occipital non-segmentation: "also known as occipitalization of the atlas [24, 27, 96]. This represents the most common anomaly involving the CVJ, encountered in 0.14-0.25% of the population [96]. Affected patients often have a low hairline, short neck, and restriction of neck movement, similar to patients with the Klippel-Feil syndrome [33]. Associated congenital abnormalities, present in approximately 20% of patients, include incomplete clefting of the nasal cartilage, cleft palate, congenital external ear deformities, cervical ribs, hypospadias, and urinary tract anomalies [33]." [p.6]
- "the C1-2 articulation becomes the sole mobile segment of the upper cervical spine, resulting in C1-2 instability in 50% of patients [96]. Up to 70% of patients may also have associated non-segmentation of C2-3 (partial or complete) [53]. Gholve et al. reported the risk of atlanto-axial instability to be particularly high in the presence of concomitant C2-3 non-segmentation [25]." [p.8]
- "Non-segmentation between the anterior atlas arch and the anterior lip of the foramen magnum often results in a characteristic 'comma-shaped' configuration to the tip of the clivus (basion) (Fig. 8). The resultant basilar invagination and dorsal displacement of the odontoid process typically result in a decreased anterior-posterior dimension of the foramen magnum and severe compression of the underlying CMJ. Sudden unexpected death associated with atlanto-occipital non-segmentation has been reported [94]." [p.8]
- Occipital condyle hypoplasia: "Clinically, condylar hypoplasia limits, or may even abolish, movement at the atlanto-occipital joint and may lead to compression of the vertebral artery as the result of excessive posterior gliding of the occiput in relation to the atlas [3]." [p.6]
- Atlas posterior arch: "Most common is the posterior arch rachischisis, present in 4% of the adult autopsy specimens [24]. Most posterior arch clefts are midline (97%)... Posterior arch rachischisis is reported to occur in 70% of children with myelomeningoceles [4]." "Partial hemiaplasias may simulate fractures on plain radiographic studies [17] (Fig. 10)." "When superimposed on the axis body or odontoid process on odontoid view plain radiographs, posterior arch rachischisis may simulate a vertical fracture." [p.8-9]
- Posterior arch hypoplasia symptoms: premature fusion of the neurocentral synchondrosis "producing spinal canal stenosis at the level of C1 has been reported in association with high cervical myelopathy [46, 90] (Fig. 11)"; Lhermitte sign, "transient quadriparesis", "chronic neck pain". [p.8-9]
- Anterior arch rachischisis: "considered rare, reportedly occurring in only 0.1% of autopsy specimens [96]." "Clefts of the anterior arch may be indicative of a dysraphic anomaly of the meninges or spinal cord and wide clefts, with only a fibrous covering, may lead to atlas instability [96]." "Of the more than 50 cases of anterior arch rachischisis we have collected, only three have been isolated, the remainder associated with some type of posterior arch anomaly." Split atlas "is stable when isolated [82, 87] and of little clinical significance." [p.10-13]. MR: "a rounded 'signal void' in place of the normal marrow signal intensity due to the presence of fibrous tissue (anterior arch pseudotumor; Fig. 14c)". "the margins of the anterior arch are not duplicated, and there is marrow signal in the arch on midsagittal MR images in the presence of the os odontoideum." [p.10]
- Odontoid hypoplasia/aplasia: "reported in association with a variety of conditions including spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, the mucopolysaccharidoses, and metatropic dwarfism. The hypoplastic odontoid is identified as a small odontoid process projecting from the superior surface of the axis body, just cephalad to the lateral articular masses with its tip lying below the anterior atlas arch. Total odontoid aplasia is extremely rare, often associated with an 'excavated' appearance to the upper margin of the axis body [95]." "In instances of both hypoplasia and aplasia, the attachments for the apical and alar ligaments are absent, predisposing to atlanto-axial instability and resultant cord compression at the level of the C1 arch [52] (Fig. 18)." [p.13-14]
- Os odontoideum: "the term os odontoideum was first introduced by Giacomini in 1886 [26]... an independent osseous structure located cephalad to the body of the axis that exists in place of the dens. A small hypoplastic odontoid process is typically present... most authors now believe that it represents an acquired condition secondary to trauma between 1 and 4 years of age [20, 21, 57, 77]. Because the gap between the os fragment and the axis body extends above the level of the superior articular facets, incompetence of the cruciate ligament and atlanto-axial instability are common, leading to severe compression of the spinal cord at the level of C1. Patients may be asymptomatic, the os discovered incidentally, or onset of symptoms may follow even minor neck trauma with neck pain, paretic episodes, or myelopathic symptoms [88]." Associations: "Down syndrome, Morquio syndrome, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, patients with the Klippel-Feil anomaly [34], and Laron syndrome [47]." [p.12]
- Persistent ossiculum terminale: "Also termed Bergman's ossicle... results from failure of fusion of the proatlas to the remainder of the odontoid process. Occasionally, this might be confused with a type I odontoid fracture (terminal ossicle avulsion), but the observation that this is a well-corticated ossicle makes a fracture unlikely (Fig. 17). Whether acquired or congenital in nature, this anomaly is stable when isolated [82, 87] and of little clinical significance." [p.12-13]
- Signs/symptoms of CVJ disease (Table 1, p.2): Motor myelopathy ("May be quite subtle and nonspecific. May be manifest only by lack of endurance"; quadriparesis, triparesis, paraparesis, hemiparesis, monoparesis); sensory (posterior column, hypalgesia, bladder dysfunction); brain stem dysfunction (nystagmus horizontal or downbeat, apnea, ataxia, dysmetria, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, facial diplegia); lower cranial nerve dysfunction (decreased hearing, dysphagia, soft palate paralysis, trapezius weakness, tongue atrophy); vascular (syncope, vertigo, intermittent paresis). "Signs and symptoms are varied, typically begin insidiously and arise fairly late, progress slowly, remain stationary, and rarely relapse." [p.1]

## Trauma & classifications
- Wackenheim line use: "very useful for assessment of CVJ traumatic injuries. If the line falls too far posterior to the odontoid, posterior craniocervical dislocation may be present and, if the lines actually intersects the body or base of the odontoid, anterior craniocervical dislocation may be diagnosed." [p.3]
- Os odontoideum versus type II odontoid fracture: "an os may be difficult to distinguish from a type II odontoid fracture in the setting of trauma. In the case of the type II odontoid fracture, the upper margin of the axis body assumes a flattened, sharp, uncorticated upper margin, and there is a normal half-moon crescentic configuration to the anterior C1 arch [37]." (os: "rounded, well-corticated structure positioned just below the basion... The anterior arch of C1 is typically hypertrophic and assumes a rounded configuration, sometimes appearing larger than the os itself.") [p.12]
- Mimics of fracture: posterior arch hemiaplasia (Fig 10), posterior arch rachischisis on odontoid view ("may simulate a vertical fracture"), "bilateral atlanto-axial offset has been reported in association with both total and partial aplasias, simulating the Jefferson fracture [24]" [p.8-9]; ossiculum terminale vs type I fracture [p.12-13].
- Grisel syndrome: "Atlanto-axial rotatory subluxation due to pharyngeal infections is commonly seen in children, termed Grisel syndrome (Fig. 23). This is thought to be caused by hyperemia at the atlanto-axial articulation during pharyngeal infections due to direct connections between pharyngovertebral veins and the periodontoid venous plexus [65]. Most atlanto-axial rotatory subluxations in children resolve spontaneously with the major prognostic factor being duration of symptoms [67]." [p.19; p.1141]
- No Anderson-D'Alonzo/Jefferson/Fielding classification is given beyond "type I", "type II" odontoid fracture names.

## Syndromic
- Klippel-Feil: "first described by Klippel and Feil in 1912 [44]. Feil went on to subdivide this syndrome into three types in 1919: Type I KFS is the classic variety with extensive cervical and thoracic segmentation anomalies, type II KFS involves segmentation anomalies of one or two pairs of cervical vertebrae, while type III KFS consists of either type I or II in conjunction with lower thoracic or lumbar anomalies [18]. The type II variant is the most common, with segmentation abnormalities most often involving C2-3 and C5-6 [32] (Fig. 19). In addition to vertebral segmentation anomalies, many CVJ anomalies are often present including basilar invagination, odontoid hypoplasia, atlanto-occipital assimilation, platybasia, and the Chiari I malformation (Fig. 19i)." [p.15; p.1137] Diastematomyelia "in 5 of 24 KFS patients"; "Hypermobility from accelerated cervical spondylosis at levels adjacent to non-segmented vertebrae may occur, and patients with KFS are prone to cervical cord insults and vertebral artery injury after even minor trauma [31, 61]." Triad: "short neck, low hairline, and limited neck mobility [44]". "More than 50% of patients have scoliosis of greater than 20°. Sprengel's deformity is present in approximately 33% of patients. Cardiovascular anomalies occur in approximately 14% of patients, ventriculoseptal defects being most common. Moore reported that 66% of patients have associated genitourinary tract abnormalities..." [p.15]
- Down syndrome: "frequently complicated by atlanto-occipital instability (AOI; Fig. 20) and atlanto-axial instability (AAI; Fig. 21) [1]. The prevalence of AAI in children with Down syndrome has been estimated at 9-30%, while the prevalence of AOI has been reported to be much higher, approximately 61% [49, 70, 80, 89, 92]." "Down syndrome children with instability have been shown to have a higher prevalence of associated osseous abnormalities compared to Down syndrome children without instability or normal children [72]. The most commonly noted osseous abnormalities include persistent synchondroses, posterior C1 rachischisis, and os odontoideum. This suggests that CVJ instability in children with Down syndrome may be secondary to a combination of ligamentous laxity and associated osseous anomalies." [p.15-17]
- Ehlers-Danlos: "characterized by a mutation, in either a collagen-forming gene or in a gene that produces a protein that processes collagen. This results in hypermobility of joints, including ligamentous laxity at the atlanto-axial joint [30] (Fig. 22)." [p.19]. Reference note: "Patients with type IV Ehlers-Danlos syndrome may be predisposed to atlantoaxial subluxation" (ref 30).
- Achondroplasia: "the most common of the short-limbed dwarfisms, is caused by a point mutation on the gene that encodes fibroblast growth factor receptor-3 [38]... autosomal dominant inheritance, although in at least 80% of patients, the disorder is caused by a spontaneous mutation. ... in the skull, the facial bones, skull base, and foramen magnum are underdeveloped, while the calvarium is normal. The constricted foramen magnum has a characteristic tear drop configuration with obliteration of the surrounding subarachnoid space resulting in compromise of the CMJ [41, 100] (Fig. 24). Other abnormalities include odontoid dysplasia, basiocciput hypoplasia, decrease in the basal angle, and thickening of the posterior rim of the foramen magnum [75] (Fig. 25)." [p.19-20; p.1141-1142]
- Mucopolysaccharidoses: "Abnormalities at the CVJ are a major cause of morbidity and mortality in certain MPS, especially Morquio (type IV) and Hurler (type I) varieties. These include a hypoplastic/dysplastic odontoid process, ligamentous instability, soft-tissue thickening around the dens, and compression of the cord by the posterior atlas arch [39] (Fig. 26). All of these lead to compromise of the spinal canal at the C1-2 level. ... The soft-tissue mass around the dens is typically hypointense or isointense on T1-weighted and hypointense on T2-weighted MR images [39]. ... some studies have shown it to represent non-ossified fibrocartilage [86]. The odontoid hypoplasia and associated soft-tissue thickening have been shown to reverse after bone marrow transplantation [36]." [p.20; p.1142]
- Osteogenesis imperfecta: "Pathology at the CVJ is most commonly seen in type IV, with a prevalence ranging from rare to 25% [83]. ... The osteoporotic bones result in repetitive microfractures causing infolding of the occipital condyles with elevation of the clivus and the posterior cranial fossa. This is accompanied by an upward migration of the cervical spine into the foramen magnum (basilar impression) producing a characteristic appearance ... described as 'tam-o'-shanter' or the 'Darth Vader' appearance (Fig. 27). The basilar impression may become symptomatic due to compression of the brain stem or lower cranial nerves [40]." [p.20]
- Abstract: "A number of syndromes are associated with CVJ pathology, the most notable being Klippel-Feil and Down syndromes, achondroplasia, the mucopolysaccharidoses, and osteogenesis imperfecta. Skull-base softening associated with some of these syndromes results in acquired basilar invagination or basilar impression." [p.1]

## Instability & management/surgical decision points
- "When identified [os odontoideum], the nature and degree of instability must be carefully assessed with flexion and extension examinations." [p.12; p.1134]
- AADI poor predictor of symptoms; neural canal width better predictor of cord compression [p.17, p.19] (quotes in GAP FILLERS). Pueschel et al. [69]: "14.6% of 404 children with Down syndrome had AADI>4.5 mm, only 1.5% of the group was symptomatic and required intervention" (1.5% is of the whole group of 404, not of the AADI>4.5 mm subset) [p.17; p.1139].
- "The need for follow-up radiographic evaluation remains controversial, as the natural evolution of AAI is unclear. While some investigators have reported decreasing prevalence of AAI with advancing age, others have reported contrary findings [7, 62, 71]." [p.19]
- Atlanto-occipital non-segmentation: C1-2 instability in 50%; up to 70% with C2-3 non-segmentation; higher instability risk with concomitant C2-3 non-segmentation [p.8].
- Clivus-canal angle <150°: "Ventral spinal cord compression may occur with angles less than 150°." [p.5]
- Prior surgery shown: "He had previously undergone posterior fossa decompression" and "Midsagittal T2-weighted MR image reveals the suboccipital craniectomy" (Fig 4 caption, p.6) - no management discussion.
- Reversibility: odontoid hypoplasia and soft-tissue thickening in MPS "reverse after bone marrow transplantation [36]" [p.20]. Rotatory subluxation in children "resolve spontaneously" [p.19].
- No fusion, decompression indications or surgical algorithm given in text.

## Urgent findings
- Cord/CMJ compression with myelopathic signs: Fig 5h ("severe compression of the brainstem... syringohydromyelic cavity"), Fig 7i, Fig 15c ("severe spinal cord compression and pathologic T2 signal within the cord"), Fig 22 ("almost complete obliteration of the spinal canal"), Fig 26b (T2 cord signal).
- Atlanto-occipital non-segmentation: "Sudden unexpected death associated with atlanto-occipital non-segmentation has been reported [94]." [p.8]
- Os odontoideum "onset of symptoms may follow even minor neck trauma" [p.12]; Klippel-Feil "prone to cervical cord insults and vertebral artery injury after even minor trauma" [p.15]; condylar hypoplasia may "lead to compression of the vertebral artery" [p.6].
- Wackenheim line intersecting dens body/base = anterior craniocervical dislocation; line too far posterior = posterior craniocervical dislocation [p.3].
- Down syndrome AOI/AAI: canal narrowing in flexion that normalizes in extension (Fig 21); clivus baseline abnormal only in extension (Fig 20) [p.16-17].
