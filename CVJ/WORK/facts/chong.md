# Facts from chong (Chong V, ed. Skull Base Imaging, Elsevier 2018; Chapter 14 "Imaging of the Craniovertebral Junction", Liu HM, Chen YF)
Page convention: [chong p.N] = PDF page N (printed book page = N + 288, e.g. PDF p.4 = printed 292). Quotes are verbatim from the text layer (hyphenation/spacing repaired).
NOTE: Chapter covers NO named classifications for Anderson-D'Alonzo (numbers only), Traynelis, Landells, Levine-Edwards/Effendi, Anderson-Montesano, Tuli/Fielding or Goel types; no ADI/BDI/Powers ratio/Redlund-Johnell/clivus-canal angle values beyond those below.

## Anatomy & ligaments
- "The craniovertebral junction (CVJ) supports the head and enables its flexion and rotation in three dimensions. It has a complex anatomic structure consisting of the vertebral column, paraspinal soft tissue, ligaments, and joints between the clivus, occipital bone, foramen magnum, atlas (C1), and axis (C2)." [chong p.3]
- Embryology: "a total of 42 somites are formed in the fourth week of gestation"; "the first four sclerotomes (first occipital, second occipital, third occipital, and fourth proatlas) ... ending in the formation of the basiocciput, exoccipital centers (i.e., the lateral margins of the foramen magnum, occipital condyles, and hypoglossal canal), jugular tubercles, posterior portions of the foramen magnum, most of the C1, and apex of the C2 dens." [chong p.3]
- "The first spinal sclerotome forms the C1 posterior arch and body of the C2 dens, whereas the second spinal sclerotome forms the body, facets, and posterior arch of the C2." [chong p.3]
- Anomalies "arise from insults occurring between the fourth and seventh week of intrauterine life." [chong p.3]
- Atlas development: "several ossification centers participate in atlas development. The lateral atlantal masses must be present at birth, and the neural ring must be formed by the age of 3 years." [chong p.6]
- Children: "the C1 body may not be visualized radiographically until patients reach 1 year of age and does not fuse until they reach 4 years of age." [chong p.13]
- "The attachments for the apical and alar ligaments are absent in aplasia and hypoplasia of the odontoid and predisposing to atlantoaxial dislocation." [chong p.9]
- Ponticulus posticus: "formed by ossification of a portion of the oblique atlantooccipital ligament"; "bony arch located between the posterior portion of the superior articular process and the posterolateral portion of the superior margin of the posterior arch of the atlas"; V3 portion of VA may pass through it. [chong p.8]
- PLL: "The posterior longitudinal ligament extends from the tectorial membrane of the basion to the posterior surface of each vertebra and disc, down to the coccyx." [chong p.16]
- Pannus: "a prolific granulation tissue mass, surrounds the odontoid process providing protection and preventing the upward motion of the dens into the irreducible basilar invagination until the individual reaches the age of 14-15 years." [chong p.7]

## Imaging technique
- "In the era of plain X-ray examination, many measurements have been proposed for evaluating the alignment of the CVJ (Table 14.1; Fig. 14.1). In this chapter, however, we emphasize the application of computed images including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and digital subtraction angiography (DSA), they still can be applied." [chong p.3]
- "Definitive diagnosis of an isolated fracture often requires CT scans (Fig. 14.11), whereas ligamentous injury is most readily identified with MRI." [chong p.12]
- "MRI is more sensitive at detecting injury to transverse ligaments." [chong p.14]
- "Vertebral artery injuries are assessable with neck computed tomography angiography (CTA) when atlas fracture is diagnosed." [chong p.14]
- Clivus fracture: "The diagnosis of clivus fracture from plain radiographs is difficult because of the overlaying dense petrous temporal bones." HRCT with multiplanar reconstruction found clivus fracture "in 9 (0.36%) of 2500 patients with head trauma". [chong p.10]
- Occipital condyle fracture: "conventional radiographs alone miss up to half of patients with acute craniocervical instability"; "requires a high index of suspicion and high-resolution tomography." [chong p.11-12]
- OPLL: "CT is best at demonstrating ossification." MRI may be unable to distinguish ossification from ligament hypertrophy "if the lesion appears as a dark signal on both T1- and T2-weighted images." [chong p.16]
- CPPD: "CT is the most sensitive modality of visualizing periodontoid calcifications". [chong p.22]
- Infection: "In evaluation of the disease extent, MRI is better since it is able to reveal osseous and soft tissue involvement and the spinal cord." [chong p.22]
- NPC: "MR is better in demonstrating mucosal masses ... parapharyngeal fat involvement, and extent of skull base infiltration and intracranial involvement." [chong p.28]
- Bow Hunter: "Provocative DSA showing that the arteries are patent in the neutral position and stenotic with head rotation is the gold standard diagnostic method (Fig. 14.28). CT and MR can detect abnormal bony structures, infarction lesions, or stenotic arteries". [chong p.32]
- DAVF: "both CT and MRI had good diagnostic performance, but the performance of MRI was slightly better than that of CT (P = .02). Contrast medium use and time-resolved MR angiography did not improve MRI diagnostic performance." [chong p.32-33]
- Foramen magnum meningioma: "Gadolinium-enhanced sequences help to delineate the dural attachment zone, the tumor, and its relation to neural and vascular structures". [chong p.29]

## Craniometry
(Table 14.1 is headed "Measurements Commonly Used in Evaluating the Craniovertebral Junction (CVJ) on Lateral Projection or Sagittal View in Images"; Fig. 14.1 landmarks: "A, posterior margin of hard palate; B, opisthion; C, inferior margin of the posterior rim of foramen magnum; D, basion; E, dorsum sella; F, anterior clinoid process; G, floor of anterior cranial fossa." Fig. 14.1 itself shows a diagram and a sagittal CT. [chong p.4])
- Chamberlain line (Palatooccipital line): A to B (hard palate posterior margin to opisthion). Normal: "Dens apex < 5 mm above this line, anterior arch of C1 typically lies below". Remark: "Diagnosis of basilar invagination. (Posterior rim of foramen magnum shows great anatomic variability and also it may be difficult to radiologically pinpoint opisthion)". Defined on lateral projection/sagittal view (radiograph era; shown on CT in Fig 14.1B). [chong p.4]
- McGregor line (Palatosuboccipital line): A to C (hard palate posterior margin to inferior margin of posterior rim of foramen magnum). Normal: "Dens apex < 7 mm above this line, anterior arch of C1 typically lies below". Remark: "Diagnosis of basilar invagination". [chong p.4]
- McRae line (Foramen magnum line): B to D (opisthion to basion). Normal: "Tip of dens below this line". Remark: "Assess the decrease in content injury" (sic). [chong p.4]
- Wackenheim line (craniovertebral or clivus-canal angle): D to E (basion to dorsum sellae, along clivus). Normal: "Line falls tangent to, or intersects, the posterior one-third of the odontoid". Remark: "Assessment of CVJ traumatic injuries". [chong p.4]
- Welcher basal angle: angle D-F-G (basion - anterior clinoid process - floor of anterior cranial fossa). Table: "<140 degrees"; "Assessment of platybasia". [chong p.4]
- Text on platybasia: "an increase in the Welcher basal angle (>143-145 degrees)". (SOURCE INCONSISTENCY with Table value <140.) [chong p.3]
- "Most patients with basilar invagination actually have a normal basal angle and thus do not have associated platybasia." [chong p.3]
- Basilar invagination definition: "the floor of the skull is pressed in by the dens and the tip of the odontoid is more cephalad and protrudes into the foramen magnum, resulting in compression of the brainstem or its neurovascular structures." [chong p.3]
- Atlantodens interval (ADI), radiography: "Normally, ADI is <3 mm in adults (<5 mm in children), but when it is 3-5 mm in adults, injury to the transverse ligament with intact alar and apical ligaments is suggested. ADI >5 mm in adults indicates serious injury to the transverse ligament, alar ligament, and tectorial membrane (atlantoaxial subluxation or dislocation)." [chong p.12-13]
- Prevertebral soft tissue: "a prevertebral soft tissue thickness of >10 mm on radiography is highly suggestive of such a fracture" (C1 anterior ring). [chong p.12]
- Rule of Spence-type sum (not named): "If the sum of lateral mass displacement is >7 mm, then a transverse ligament rupture is assured and the injury pattern is considered unstable." (Modality not stated; context CT/radiograph of atlas fracture.) [chong p.14]
- Predental space on lateral radiograph (anterior arch cleft): "the predental space sits between the anterior arch of the atlas (which appears to be crescent, or half-moon, shaped) and the dens. In the anterior arch cleft, the predental space is obscured by the anterior arch, which becomes fatty and round and appears to overlap the dens." [chong p.8]
- Distance used in Fig 14.7: line between upper margins of anterior and posterior arches of C1 used to follow dens tip ascent (no value given). [chong p.10]
- Degenerative: "with age, the ADI narrowed linearly" (CT, 1543 adults). [chong p.16]
- Imaging criteria for the two basilar invagination types: violation of "the Chamberlain, McRae, and Wackenheim lines" (classic) versus "violation of the Chamberlain line but not the McRae and Wackenheim lines" with horizontal clivus (second type). [chong p.5]

## Congenital/developmental
- Box 14.1 lists: occipital bone (platybasia; basilar invagination; condylar hypoplasia; condylar dysplasia (condylus tertius, paracondyloid process, basilar process)); atlas (assimilation; aplasia/hypoplasia; arch anomaly; ponticulus posticus); axis/odontoid (aplasia/hypoplasia of dens; persistent ossiculum terminale; os odontoideum; Klippel-Feil). [chong p.5]
- Platybasia: "flattening of the skull base resulting in an increase in the Welcher basal angle (>143-145 degrees). Platybasia alone does not usually cause symptoms unless it is associated with basilar invagination or other anomalies." [chong p.3]
- Basilar invagination types: "divided by etiology into two types: primary or developmental invagination, which is more common, and secondary or acquired invagination." Developmental is associated with "atlantooccipital assimilation, hypoplasia of the basioccipital or atlas, incomplete fusion of the posterior arch of the atlas, odontoid anomalies, Klippel-Feil syndrome, and vertebral artery anomalies. In about 25%-35% of cases, basilar invagination is associated with Arnold-Chiari malformation, syringomyelia, and hydrocephalus." Symptoms "usually occur in the second or third decade. The most common local finding is short neck and torticollis." [chong p.4-5]
- Two imaging groups (attribution to Goel/Menezes not stated in chapter text [UNVERIFIED]; presented as authors' observation): group 1 "odontoid process protrusion into the foramen magnum in the cephalad direction, C1-2 dislocation or dens-clivus dislocation causing direct compression of the pontomedullary junction with violation of the Chamberlain, McRae, and Wackenheim lines, and listhesis of the oblique C1 and C2 facets"; group 2 "horizontal orientation of the clivus and anatomic alignment of the odontoid process and clivus despite the presence of basilar invagination, violation of the Chamberlain line but not the McRae and Wackenheim lines, and normal or fused horizontal C1-2 facet joints ... related to atlantoaxial dislocation stemming from aplasia and hypoplasia of the atlas." [chong p.5]
- Acquired basilar invagination causes: "general osteopenia (Paget disease, osteomalacia, renal osteodystrophy, and hyperparathyroidism), local bone destruction (from a primary or secondary tumor or infection), trauma, and systematic diseases such as osteogenesis imperfecta, achondrodysplasia, osteopetrosis, mucopolysaccharidosis, and cretinism." [chong p.5]
- Reducible to irreducible progression: "In the initial stage, atlantoaxial instability is reducible. The pannus ... preventing the upward motion of the dens into the irreducible basilar invagination until the individual reaches the age of 14-15 years. However, compression of the cervicomedullary junction may cause the clinical symptoms and signs." [chong p.7-8]
- Irreducible basilar invagination: "the CVJ emerges from the abnormal groove behind the occipital condyles and pushes up into the cranial base, resulting in platybasia and a short horizontal clivus. The upward excursion of the cranial base and the decrease of the vertical height of the posterior fossa leads to an acquired hindbrain herniation syndrome. The treatment of reducible, irreducible, and acquired hindbrain herniation syndrome are completely different." [chong p.8]
- Condylar hypoplasia: "skull base is flattened or even ascends medially, leading to basilar invagination and widening of the atlantooccipital joint axis angle"; "may lead to compression of the vertebral artery because of posterior sliding of the occiput in relation to the atlas." [chong p.5]
- Condylar dysplasia (condylus tertius): "persistence of the proatlas ... or its failed integration into the occipital bone"; "usually directly above and anterior to the arch of C1 and may form a joint or pseudojoint with the clivus, odontoid process, or anterior arch of C1". [chong p.5]
- Atlas assimilation: "complete or partial congenital fusion of C1 with the occiput", from failure of segmentation between fourth occipital and first spinal sclerotome; "Bilateral atlas assimilation invariably leads to basilar invagination." With Klippel-Feil, esp. C2-3, "there is a gradual loosening of the atlantoaxial joint, and the atlantoaxial subsequently becomes unstable. Sudden death has been reported". [chong p.5-6]
- Atlas aplasia/hypoplasia: "Most isolated atlas anomalies are benign except the atlantal assimilations"; abnormal atlantal development seen in "spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, achondroplasia, Goldenhar syndrome" and "Down syndrome". [chong p.6]
- Persistent bifid arches beyond 3-4 years in skeletal dysplasias, MPS, Down, Goldenhar, Conradi, Morquio and atlas assimilation; "presentation is torticollis and plagiocephaly. Without proper management, this subsequently results in atlantoaxial subluxation (AAS)." [chong p.7]
- Atlas arch clefts: "posterior clefts being the most common and apparent in 4% of adults. The majority of posterior atlas clefts (97%) are midline. Bilateral atlas posterior clefts may resemble the Jefferson fracture. Anterior arch clefts are quite rare (0.1%) and usually associated with posterior clefts (split atlas). In Down syndrome, split atlas occurs frequently and is associated with os odontoideum and the subsequent formation of AAS." [chong p.8]
- Ponticulus posticus: "The incidence ... is about 8% (range 2.6%-14.3%), and it is slightly more common in females"; complete, incomplete or calcified; "may cause chronic tension-type headache, orofacial pain, and migraine, and its relationship to Bow hunter syndrome (BHS) is unclear." [chong p.8]
- Dens aplasia: "Total aplasia of the odontoid process is extremely rare"; associated with "Morquio syndrome, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, and metatrophic dwarfism". [chong p.8-9]
- Persistent ossiculum terminale (Bergmann): "nonfusion of the terminal ossification center with the remnant of the odontoid process... has to be differentiated from type I odontoid fracture (avulsion of the terminal ossicle). The odontoid process is usually of normal height. This anomaly is stable when isolated and of little clinical significance." [chong p.9]
- Os odontoideum: "an independent bone lying cranially to the C2 body in place of the dens"; "smooth borders and a small dens is always present"; "gap ... usually extends above the level of the superior facets of the C2". Type A orthotopic ("relocation of the os odontoideum to the site of the dens and movement of the os odontoideum with the C1 and C2 vertebrae"); type B dystopic ("relocation ... to a site near the skull base and movement ... with the clivus"). "The entire complex leads to an incompetence of the cruciate ligament and, subsequently, to atlantoaxial instability. In extreme cases, the os odontoideum may become fixed and associated with severe basilar invagination and compression of the cervicomedullary junction." [chong p.9]
- Klippel-Feil: "congenital fusion or malsegmentation of the cervical spine ... between the third and eighth weeks of gestation"; localized, multisegmental or diffuse; "Less than 50% of cases present with the classic triad of short neck, webbed neck, and low hairline." [chong p.9]

## Trauma & classifications
- Clivus fracture: "incidence of 0.21%-0.56% among patients with head trauma"; longitudinal, transverse or oblique; cranial nerve VI, VII deficits; "Mortality is high (24%-31%) because of brain stem trauma and carotid or vertebrobasilar occlusion. Transverse and diastatic fractures are associated with a higher mortality rate and worse outcome." [chong p.10-11]
- Occipital condyle fracture (Box 14.2; no author named): "Type 1 fractures result from axial compression with comminution of the occipital condyle, and the intact alar ligaments and tectorial membrane tend to stabilize the craniovertebral junction (CVJ). Type 2 fractures result from a direct blow to the skull base and occipital condyle, and the intact alar ligaments tend to stabilize the CVJ. Type 3 fractures result from avulsion, and the torn alar ligaments tend to destabilize the CVJ." [chong p.13]
- "Occipital condyle fracture should be suspected when paralysis of the 9th through the 12th nerves (Collet-Sicard syndrome) occurs after minor head trauma." Incidence of craniocervical injury "might be as high as 16%". [chong p.11]
- Atlas fractures: "account for 2%-13% of acute injuries of the cervical spine ... due to a traumatic axial load, and are typically associated with other damage to the upper cervical spine." [chong p.12]
- Box 14.3 (atlas fractures by pattern): "Type I—fractures that are limited to the anterior or posterior arch, with intact transverse ligament; Type II—fractures (also known as Jefferson fractures) that typically consist of bilateral burst fractures through the anterior and posterior arches of C1; Type III—fractures that involve the lateral mass". "Jefferson fractures (type II) and lateral mass fractures (type III) may occur in isolation or in conjunction with significant ligamentous disruption." [chong p.13]
- Vertebral artery: "If a vertebral artery dissection is found, the patient will need anticoagulation with heparin and further evaluation by interventional radiology." [chong p.14]
- Odontoid fracture (Box 14.4, numbered types, no eponym in box; ref 15 Anderson & D'Alonzo is cited for os terminale): "Type I—an oblique fracture through the upper part of the odontoid process; Type II—a fracture occurring at the base of the odontoid as it attaches to the body of C2; Type III—a fracture that extends through the body of the axis". Incidence "approaches 15% of all C-spine fractures". Type I "(less than 5% of cases) usually is stable if limited to an avulsion injury to the tip of the odontoid process. Occasionally, the apical and/or alar ligaments are injured ... and become unstable." "More than 60% of odontoid fractures are type II fractures". Type III "(30% of cases) can extend laterally to the superior articular facet of the atlas and develop vertically in the odontoid process and body of the axis (less than 5% of cases)." [chong p.14]
- Fig 14.13 note: "on the sagittal view alone, sometimes, it is difficult to differentiate the types of fracture." [chong p.15]
- Hangman fracture: "involves the pars interarticularis and adjacent structures and results from hyperextension injury." Box 14.5: "Type 1—an isolated minimally displaced fracture of the ring without displacement of the body or facets; Type 2—a fracture of the pars with anterior displacement of the axial body; Type 3—a fracture of the pars with anterior displacement of the axial body in the direction of flexion and dislocation of the bilateral facets". "An isolated C2 lateral mass fracture is extremely rare." [chong p.14, 16]

## Rheumatoid/inflammatory
- "patients with RA for more than 7 years develop craniocervical complications in 30%-50% of cases, whereas patients with RA for more than 14 years develop AAS with myelopathy in 2.5% of cases." [chong p.19]
- RA: pannus; "erosion may give the dens of the atlantoaxial joint a 'pencil-tip' appearance"; "Inflammation leads to weakening of the transverse and alar ligaments, and AAS can occur." "AAS with severe occipitalgia or high cervical myelopathy is the most common type of instability of the upper cervical spine in patients with RA. Lateral subluxation might also result in vertebral artery compression and then vertebrobasilar infarctions." [chong p.19-20]
- RA CT: "an uncalcified pannus surrounds and erodes the dens. There are also erosions of the uncovertebral and facet joints." MRI: pannus "low signal on T1-weighted image, heterogeneous signal on T2-weighted image (usually intermediate to low signal), and enhancement by injection of contrast medium". [chong p.20]
- Ankylosing spondylitis: "best diagnostic clue is fusion between vertebral bodies"; "90% of patients are HLA-B27 positive"; AAS "found to be a frequent complication in 14.1% of patients with AS, regardless of cervical symptoms"; "likely secondary to subaxial fusion". [chong p.20-21]
- CPPD ("crowned dens", Bouvet 1985): CT linear calcific deposits in transverse ligament, alar, apical ligaments, superior longitudinal fibers of cruciate ligament; usually >50 years; can present as cystic retroodontoid mass. [chong p.18, 21-22]
- Dialysis/amyloid: "ROPs ... caused by amyloid deposits"; amyloid appears "hypointense to muscle on both T1- and T2-weighted images". [chong p.18]
- Retroodontoid mass should be differentiated from "CPPD disease, amyloid arthropathy, and pseudotumors of the CVJ attributable to chronic mechanical instability." [chong p.20]

## Infection incl TB
- Pyogenic infection "more commonly seen in cases of contiguous infection"; "CVJ TB is a rare infection (occurring in only 0.3%-1% of spinal TB cases), usually secondary to a primary focus... The ligaments are involved secondarily by displacement and disruption. The infection usually starts from the lateral joint and spreads to the midline and then to the contralateral joint in the late phase. Tissue biopsy is imperative to confirm the diagnosis". [chong p.22]
- Imaging: "bone erosion and ligament destruction leading to instability at the CVJ and phlegmon or abscess formation in the paravertebral and epidural regions"; marrow "hypointensity on T1-weighted image and hyperintensity on T2-weighted image"; "heterogeneous enhancement (in phlegmons) or peripheral enhancement with central nonenhancing pus (in abscesses)". [chong p.22]
- Osteoradionecrosis: ORN "tends to be more contiguous in bone and intervening joint space, more bilateral, and more destructive of facet joints and subchondral bone. Tumor recurrence or metastasis at the CVJ tends to involve the posterior arch, tends to destroy lateral border bone, and tends to have extraosseous soft tissue mass". [chong p.22-23]
- No tuberculosis classification described in text (Chatterjee & Das 2015 "proposal for a new classification" appears only in reference list, ref 56). [chong p.39]

## Neoplasm
- "The role of imaging is to determine the nature and resectability of the tumors." Evaluate "tumor extent, direction of encroachment, whether the lesion has a vascular or intramedullary component, and ... craniovertebral stability." [chong p.24]
- Chordoma: CT "well-defined expansile soft tissue mass that arises from the clivus with associated lytic bone change"; T1 "intermediate to low signal"; T2 "bright signal"; "moderate to marked enhancement". [chong p.25]
- Chondrosarcoma: "typically located in the paramedian petroclival synchondrosis"; "an off-midline growth suggests chondrosarcoma"; DWI: "chondrosarcomas showing higher mean apparent diffusion coefficient values". [chong p.26-27]
- Fibrous dysplasia: "ground-glass" on CT; osteoblastoma: "classic benign features on CT" avoid misdiagnosis/aggressive resection. [chong p.27-28]
- ABC: "About 10%-20% of ABCs occur in the spine, and 2% occur in the C-spine... About 80% ... less than 20 years old." [chong p.28]
- Plasmacytoma: "homogeneous signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted images and usually lower signal intensity on T2-weighted images". [chong p.28]
- NPC: "In cases of CVJ bone lesion, the nasopharyngeal mucosa should always be carefully checked, because a small mucosal tumor could be overlooked." [chong p.28]
- Meningioma at foramen magnum: "approximately 3% of all meningiomas occur at the foramen magnum"; "Ten percent have extradural extensions"; higher incomplete removal for extradural; "Tumors with adherence to the brainstem, encasement of the vertebral arteries and/or cranial nerves, en plaque growth pattern, high tumor grade, and high mitotic activity have been associated with an increased rate of incomplete resection and/or tumor recurrence. Radiosurgery is an alternative treatment". [chong p.28-29]
- Nerve sheath tumors: "70% of cases are intradural extramedullary, 15% are completely extradural, and 10% are transforaminal"; neurofibromas "more likely to show the target sign". [chong p.30]
- Congenital cysts: epidermoid shows "restricted diffusion"; neurenteric cysts "no enhancement". [chong p.30-31]

## Syndromic (Down, Morquio, achondroplasia etc.)
- "Down syndrome is associated with a 14%-20% incidence of atlantoaxial dislocation." [chong p.7]
- Down: split atlas, os odontoideum, AAS. [chong p.8]
- Morquio: odontoid aplasia; persistent bifid atlas arch. [chong p.7, 9]
- Achondroplasia/MPS/osteogenesis imperfecta/osteopetrosis/cretinism: acquired basilar invagination. [chong p.5]
- Vertebral AV fistula spontaneous with "fibromuscular dysplasia, neurofibromatosis, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and Marfan syndrome". [chong p.32]

## Degenerative/other
- Atlantoaxial OA: "underrecognized source of neck pain, limitation of range of motion, and cervicogenic headaches"; prevalence "severe lateral atlantoaxial osteoarthritis ranges from 5.4% in the sixth to 18.2% in the ninth decade"; CT 1543 adults: "osteoarthritis of the anterior atlantodens joint in 44.6% ... 1.4% in the youngest group to 92.9% in the oldest"; "intraosseous cysts ... from 4.2% to 37.4%"; "calcific synovitis ... 0% to 11.1%". Imaging: "marginal osteophyte formation, joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, and subchondral cyst formation". [chong p.14-16]
- OPLL: "after the age of 50 years"; "most commonly ... C4-5"; CT "upside-down T" or "bowtie"; "MRI: hypointense on T1 and T2". [chong p.16]
- DISH: "thick flowing ossification of the anterior longitudinal ligaments ... separated from their vertebral bodies by relatively normal intervertebral discs and facet joints." [chong p.16]
- Retroodontoid pseudotumor: "nonneoplastic mass"; "MRI shows hypointensity on both T1-weighted and T2-weighted images with peripheral enhancement"; "Goel suggested for the first time that retroodontoid tissue is a manifestation of atlantoaxial instability". [chong p.17-18]
- Bow Hunter syndrome: dynamic VA occlusion with rotation, atlantoaxial type vs subaxial type; "Compression in BHS has been most commonly observed at or above the C2 level". [chong p.32]
- VA aneurysm at CVJ: "hemiplegia cruciata". [chong p.32]
- VAVF Kondoh Box 14.6: "Type 1 lesions receive retrograde flow from the parent vertebral artery. Type 2 ... from the contralateral vertebral artery. Type 3 lesions are supplied from the contralateral vertebral artery and concurrent steal from the internal carotid arteries via basilar artery retrograde flow into the parent vertebral artery." [chong p.34]
- DAVF: foramen magnum DAVF "subarachnoid hemorrhage in 34%-45% of cases"; "The venous drainage pattern is the most important factor that influences the clinical presentation." Anterior condylar DAVF can mimic carotid-cavernous fistula; "fast flow has a bright signal intensity on MRA". [chong p.33-34]

## Instability & management/surgical decision points
- "The treatment of reducible, irreducible, and acquired hindbrain herniation syndrome are completely different." [chong p.8]
- Degenerative CVJ arthritis: "Surgical decompression and atlantoaxial fusion might be considered for managing refractory cases." [chong p.16]
- ROP: "proposed surgical procedures focused on atlantoaxial fixation to relieve this instability. Subsequently, several authors performed atlantoaxial fixation for such lesions and demonstrated resolution of the retroodontoid mass. However, some of the lesions were not associated with instability." [chong p.17-18]
- Transverse ligament: ADI >5 mm adults / lateral mass sum >7 mm = unstable (see Craniometry). [chong p.12-14]
- Occipital condyle fracture classification states ligament status (alar/tectorial) governing stability (Box 14.2). [chong p.13]
- Foramen magnum DAVF: "treatment by direct microsurgery interruption of the arterialized venous circulation may be preferable to arterial embolization." [chong p.34]
- Meningioma: radiosurgery for advanced age/high risk, residual, small lesions. [chong p.29]
- Tumor imaging: "evaluate craniovertebral stability". [chong p.24]

## Urgent findings
- Odontoid type I may become unstable with apical/alar ligament injury. [chong p.14]
- ADI >5 mm adults: "serious injury"; lateral mass sum >7 mm: transverse ligament rupture. [chong p.13-14]
- Atlas fracture with transverse foramen fracture: CTA for VA injury; dissection needs heparin. [chong p.14]
- Occipital condyle type III avulsion: destabilizes CVJ. [chong p.13]
- Clivus fracture mortality 24%-31%. [chong p.10-11]
- Cord compression by retroodontoid mass, RA AAS with myelopathy. [chong p.17-19]
- DAVF with cortical venous reflux: "aggressive lesions". [chong p.32-33]
- Bilateral atlas assimilation + Klippel-Feil: instability, "Sudden death has been reported". [chong p.5-6]
